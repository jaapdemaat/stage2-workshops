Unit 5

In Unit 5 you reference other people’s work twice: in your 600-word Midpoint draft and in your 2,500-word designed critical report. Both must use Harvard style, in the version set out by Cite Them Right. It’s the referencing style UAL uses for all taught courses.

You’ll find many versions of Harvard online, and they differ in small details such as punctuation and italics. Only use the Cite Them Right version.

Why we reference

Your critical report puts your practice in conversation with other people’s ideas, images and work. Referencing is how you show that conversation honestly.

It gives credit

Someone made the book, the poster or the idea you’re building on. Naming them is a matter of respect, and of academic integrity.

It shows your thinking

Your references are a map of who you’re thinking with. They show your tutors the research behind your position.

It lets others follow

A good reference lets your reader find the source, check it, and take it further than you did.

One day it might be your work in someone else’s reference list: a student’s critical report, a book, an exhibition text. You’d want them to get your name, your title and your year right. Do the same for the people you reference.

Referencing is typography

Italics, quotation marks, brackets, a comma in exactly the right place: Harvard is a small type system with its own style sheet. Set it well and nobody notices. Set it badly and everyone does, just like kerning on a headline. 🔍

It has two parts that always work together: a short citation in your text, and a full reference in your reference list at the end.

Citing in your text

Author, year, and a page number (or a timestamp for video and audio) when you need it. Put the author’s name in your sentence, or the whole citation in brackets. Both are right. Harvard doesn’t use footnotes.

Quoting
  • Short quotes (up to two or three lines) go in quotation marks in your sentence. Single or double — just be consistent.
  • Longer quotes go in their own indented paragraph, with no quotation marks.
  • Always give the page number. Quotes count towards your word count, and your tutors want to read your interpretation, so use them sparingly.

Good to know: your in-text citations count towards your 2,500 words. Your reference list and bibliography don’t.

Reference list & bibliography

A reference list lists everything you cited. A bibliography also includes background reading you didn’t cite.

Unit 5

Your critical report ends with two lists, both alphabetical and both in Cite Them Right Harvard:

  • Reference list: every source you cite in your text.
  • Bibliography: background reading that shaped your work but that you don’t cite.
  • Order: alphabetical by author surname. No author? Use the title. No author or title? Use the URL.
  • Names: surname first, then initials — Malpass, M.
  • Four or more authors: first author, then et al.
  • Every detail counts: Cite Them Right asks you to follow each pattern exactly, including capital letters, typeface and punctuation.
What goes first?

Every reference starts with whoever made the source. Try an example:

  1. Is there a named person?Surname, then initials.
  2. No person, but an organisation?Use the organisation.
  3. No author at all?Use the title, in italics. Never “Anon”.
  4. No author and no title?Use the URL, and ask yourself whether it’s a reliable source.

📖Book

PatternSurname, I. (Year) Title. Edition (only if not the first). Publisher.ExampleVis, D. (2021) Research for people who (think they) would rather create. Onomatopee.

📚Edited book

PatternSurname, I. (ed.) (Year) Title. Edition. Publisher.

Two or more editors? Write (eds) instead of (ed.).

Example from Cite Them RightZurbriggen, E.L. and Capdevila, R. (eds) (2024) The Palgrave handbook of power, gender, and psychology. Palgrave Macmillan.

📑Chapter in an edited book

PatternSurname, I. (Year) ‘Title of chapter’, in I. Surname (ed.), Title of book. Publisher, pp. X–Y.

Reference the person who wrote the chapter, not the editor. A chapter scanned onto Moodle is referenced the same way.

Example from Cite Them RightCole, E.R. (2023) ‘Beyond identity: intersectionality and power’, in E.L. Zurbriggen and R. Capdevila (eds), The Palgrave handbook of power, gender, and psychology. Palgrave Macmillan, pp. 29–42.

📰Journal article (print)

PatternSurname, I. (Year) ‘Title of article’, Title of Journal, Volume(Issue), pp. X–Y.ExampleMalpass, M. (2015) ‘Criticism and function in critical design practice’, Design Issues, 31(2), pp. 59–71.

🔗Journal article (online)

With a DOISurname, I. (Year) ‘Title of article’, Title of Journal, Volume(Issue), pp. X–Y. Available at: DOIWith a URL (no DOI)Surname, I. (Year) ‘Title of article’, Title of Journal, Volume(Issue), pp. X–Y. Available at: URL (Accessed: date).

A DOI never changes, so it doesn’t need an access date.

Example from Cite Them RightVan Dis, E.A.M. et al. (2023) ‘ChatGPT: five priorities for research’, Nature, 614(7947), pp. 224–226. Available at: https://doi.org/10.1038/d41586-023-00288-7

🗞️Magazine article

PatternSurname, I. (Year) ‘Title of article’, Title of Magazine, Issue information, Page reference. Online: add Available at: URL (Accessed: date).ExampleKhandwala, A. (2019) ‘What does it mean to decolonize design?’, Eye on Design (5 June). Available at: https://eyeondesign.aiga.org/what-does-it-mean-to-decolonize-design/ (Accessed: 26 November 2026).

🌐Web page by an organisation

PatternOrganisation (Year published or last updated) Title of web page. Available at: URL (Accessed: date).

No date on the page? Write (no date). Always add the date you accessed it, because web pages change. Don’t put the URL in your in-text citation, only in your reference list.

ExampleDesign Justice Network (no date) Design Justice Network. Available at: https://designjustice.org/ (Accessed: 26 November 2026).

▶️Online video (YouTube, Vimeo)

PatternName of person or organisation posting the video (Year posted) Title of film or programme. Date uploaded. Available at: URL (Accessed: date).

The author is whoever posted the video, which isn’t always the person who made it. To point to a moment, add the time: (Surname, Year, 27:45).

Example from Cite Them RightCambridge Cosmology (2021) Professor Gabriella González: Black holes and gravitational waves. 12 January. Available at: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Txuq1sO8fkY (Accessed: 5 May 2021).

📱Instagram post

PatternSurname, I. (Year posted) ‘Title of post’ [Instagram]. Day Month. Available at: URL (Accessed: date).

For images you saw on social media, use the pattern for photographs in online collections.

Example from Cite Them RightTusk, D. (2018) ‘A piece of cake perhaps?’ [Instagram]. 20 September. Available at: https://www.instagram.com/p/Bn8Luwbjzf9/ (Accessed: 7 February 2019).

🏷️Logo

PatternDesigner or organisation (Date) Title of logo [Logo]. Available at: URL (Accessed: date).Example from Cite Them RightCrosby, F.G. (1919) Winged B [Logo]. Available at: https://www.bentleymotors.com/en/about-bentley/history-and-heritage.html (Accessed: 23 May 2024).

🪧Poster

PatternSurname, I. (Year) Title [Poster]. Exhibited at location, date(s) of exhibition. Dimensions.

Leave out what you don’t know: not every poster was exhibited or has known dimensions. No date? Write (no date).

Example from Cite Them RightSmith, K. (2003) Prints, books and things [Poster]. Exhibited at New York, Museum of Modern Art. 5 December 2003 to 8 March 2004.

📷Photo or image online (incl. Instagram, Pinterest)

PatternPhotographer (Year) Title of photograph [Medium]. Available at: URL (Accessed: date).

Cite where you saw it. The medium can be [Photograph], or the platform, e.g. [Instagram] or [Pinterest]. No photographer? Start with the account name or the title.

Example from Cite Them RightConway, D. (1991) Adzido Pan African dance [Photograph]. Available at: https://www.bridgemaneducation.com/en/asset/3890655/ (Accessed: 13 June 2024).

🏛️Exhibition

PatternTitle of exhibition (Year) [Exhibition]. Location. Dates of exhibition.Artist knownArtist (Year) Title of exhibition [Exhibition]. Location. Dates of exhibition.

Online exhibition? Write [Online exhibition] and add Available at: URL (Accessed: date).

Example from Cite Them RightTuulikki, H. (2021) SING SIGN: a close duet [Online exhibition]. National Galleries of Scotland, Edinburgh. 3 May–13 June 2021. Available at: https://www.nationalgalleries.org/exhibition/hanna-tuulikki-sing-signclose-duet (Accessed: 3 June 2021).

🎨Painting or drawing seen in person

PatternSurname, I. (Year of production) Title of work [Medium]. Location of the work (Viewed: date).Example from Cite Them RightMartin, J. (1817) The bard [Painting]. Laing Art Gallery, Newcastle upon Tyne (Viewed: 21 February 2024).

🖼️Work of art seen online

PatternSurname, I. (Year) Title of work [Medium]. Available at: URL (Accessed: date).

The website takes the place of the gallery.

Example from Cite Them RightGormley, A. (1998) Angel of the North [Sculpture]. Available at: https://newcastlegateshead.com/business-directory/things-to-do/angel-of-the-north (Accessed: 6 June 2024).

🎬Film seen at the cinema

PatternTitle of film (Year of distribution) Directed by I. Surname. [Feature film]. Distribution company.

In your text, cite a film by its title and year: (Title of film, Year).

Example from Cite Them RightHoard (2023) Directed by L. Carmoon. [Feature film]. Erebus Pictures.

🎤Lecture you attended (in person or on Teams)

PatternSurname, I. (Year) ‘Title of lecture’ [Lecture]. Unit code: Unit title. Institution. Day Month.Example from Cite Them RightMay, P. (2024) ‘Banking regulation’ [Lecture]. BUS316: BSc Economics. Northumbria University. 21 February.

For a Unit 5 lecture, the unit code and title are MU001494: GCD Practices 03.

💻Recorded lecture (on Moodle)

PatternSurname, I. (Year) ‘Title of lecture’ [Recorded lecture]. Unit code: Unit title. Institution. Day Month. Available at: URL of the Moodle page (Accessed: date).

Only for recordings behind a login. A public recording follows the online video pattern.

Example from Cite Them RightBrown, T. (2024) ‘Bridge construction techniques’ [Recorded lecture]. ENG1145: BEng. Durham University. 21 March. Available at: https://blackboard.durham.ac.uk/ultra/stream (Accessed: 21 April 2024).

Images in your designed report

Your critical report is a designed document, so images are part of your argument. They need crediting just like text.

Where the image came fromWhat to do
A figure, table or illustration in a book or articleCite the book or article, with the page and figure number: (Strong, 1990, p. 62, Fig. 12). Your bibliography lists the whole book or article.
A photo or image online, or on social mediaReference it where you saw it, using the photo or image online card.
An image you found through GoogleClick through to the page it actually comes from and reference that. No photographer? Use the title, in italics.
An image you made with AIGive it a caption and a citation with the AI company and year, linked to your reference list. See AI tools.

Captions and figure numbers

Every image in your critical report gets a number and a caption. In Unit 5, follow Cite Them Right’s guidance:

  1. Number your images in the order they appear: Figure 1, Figure 2, Figure 3…
  2. Write a caption that starts with “Figure”, then the number, then a description.
  3. Someone else’s image? End the caption with the source, in round brackets: (Source: …).
  4. Point to it in your writing by its number, e.g. (Figure 1).

AI tools

UAL asks you to be open about any AI help you use. If you use AI in your work:

  • Reference any AI you use, just like any other source, using the Cite Them Right patterns below.
  • Keep a record of how you used it, following the UAL Student Guide to Generative AI →. Keep it until your work has been assessed.
  • Save a copy of any AI conversation only you can see. Your tutor may ask for it as an appendix.
  • Don’t submit AI-generated work unaltered as your own. It may count as academic misconduct through plagiarism.
  • Check every reference an AI gives you against the library. Some will be made up.

For translation tools, see Writing in English as an additional language in Additional Support.

FAQ

Quick answers to the questions students ask most. Answers come from Cite Them Right’s common questions and guidance pages.

Do I always need a page number?

Always for a direct quote. Also when you use an idea from a specific page. If you’re referring to a whole book, author and year are enough: (Vis, 2021).

How do I quote a web page that has no page numbers?

Use the paragraph number, or count paragraphs down from the top: (Surname, Year, para. 11).

I found an image on Google. Who do I credit?

Click through to the page the image actually comes from, and reference that. No photographer named? Use the title, in italics. Never write “Anon”.

Which edition do I reference?

The one you actually used. If you read the 1960 edition, reference 1960, even if a newer one exists.

How do I reference a PDF?

Work out what the PDF actually is: a book, a journal article, a magazine article, a report. Then use the pattern for that.

What if a source was quoted in the book I’m reading?

Try to find and read the original. If you can’t, write e.g. Harvey (2015, quoted in Lewis, 2018, p. 86). Only Lewis, the book you read, goes in your reference list.

Two authors have the same surname and initials. What do I do?

Add their first names in the citation: (Richards, Patricia, 2018) and (Richards, Peter, 2018).

I’m citing several pages from the same organisation, and none have a date.

Add letters: (Organisation, no date - a), (Organisation, no date - b). Each page gets its own entry in your reference list.

Can I use a web page with no author, title or date?

Cite Them Right advises against it for academic work. If you can’t tell who made it or when, you probably can’t rely on it.

An AI gave me some references. Can I use them?

Check every one against the library first. AI tools sometimes invent references that don’t exist.

Is there a tool that can build my reference list?

Yes: reference management software. The UAL Library referencing guide explains how to use it. Always check what it produces against Cite Them Right.
